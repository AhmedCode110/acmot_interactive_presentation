Section I · Evaluation metrics

IoU: How Much Do Two Boxes Overlap?

IoU in one line

0 = the boxes do not touch · 1 = identical boxes. A prediction usually counts as correct when IoU ≥ 0.5.

IoU — evaluation

Compares a prediction with the ground truth to decide TP or FP.

NMS — inside the detector

Compares two predictions and deletes the duplicate if they overlap too much.

Same overlap formula, two different jobs. Later we will also meet a third use: the tracker's matching threshold.
Illustration

Say: "IoU is overlap divided by union. Zero means no overlap, one means a perfect match. The same idea is used by NMS inside the detector to remove duplicate boxes."

Caveat to mention: IoU for evaluation, NMS IoU for duplicate removal, and the tracker's matching threshold are three different settings. AC-MOT only adapts the NMS one.

Precision

Of all predicted objects, how many were correct?

Recall

Of all real objects, how many did we find?

Small example
Precision = 6 ⁄ 8 = 0.75
Recall = 6 ⁄ 10 = 0.60
A detector can trade one for the other. A lower confidence threshold usually raises recall but adds false positives.
Illustrative example numbers

Say: "Precision asks: of the boxes I drew, how many were right? Recall asks: of the real objects, how many did I find? Here 6 of 8 predictions are right, so precision is 0.75; 6 of 10 cars are found, so recall is 0.60."

Link: the confidence threshold moves this trade-off — and AC-MOT controls that threshold.

Section I · Tracking metrics

ID Switches and IDF1: Keeping the Right Name

One object followed over four frames
IDS · lower is better

Counts the jumps

Every time the ID changes to a wrong one, IDS goes up by one. Here the object switched away and back.

IDF1 · higher is better

Counts the time

IDF1 = 2·IDTP ⁄ (2·IDTP + IDFP + IDFN)

The share of time an object carries its correct identity — here about 3 of 4 frames.

Why both

Two different views

IDS says how often identity breaks. IDF1 says how long identity stays right. MOTA mostly ignores both.

Illustration

Say: "Object A is ID 7, then briefly becomes ID 12, then returns. IDS counts that break. IDF1 measures how much of the time the identity was correct — about three out of four frames here."

Caveat: the exact IDS counting rule depends on the evaluator (TrackEval counts a switch when the matched ID changes). The picture is simplified.

Section IV · SCI cue 1 of 5

Crowd Density: How Many Objects?

c = min( number of previous boxes ⁄ 30 , 1 )

Uses the boxes from the previous detector pass. 30 or more boxes counts as fully crowded.

3 objects

c = 0.10

low crowd

15 objects

c = 0.50

medium crowd

30+ objects

c = 1.00

fully crowded (capped)

Crowding causes more overlap, hidden objects, and ID switches. Later, V1/V2 used a percentile rank from validation data instead of “÷ 30”.

Say: "The first cue counts boxes from the previous frame and divides by 30. Three boxes gives 0.1, fifteen gives 0.5, thirty or more is capped at 1."

Honest caveat: 30 was an engineering choice. In the defensible V1 and V2 studies, this cue became a percentile rank calibrated from the fixed detector's own box counts on validation data — so no ground truth leaks in.

Section IV · SCI cue 2 of 5

Tiny-Object Ratio: How Small Are They?

t = (boxes with area < 32 × 32 px) ⁄ (all previous boxes)
t = 7 ⁄ 10 = 0.70

Why tiny objects are hard

  • Very few pixels to recognise
  • Lost first when the input image is small
  • Low confidence scores
  • A small shift breaks IoU matching

Why 32 × 32?

It is the common “small object” size used in detection benchmarks such as COCO. It was not learned by the optimizer.

In aerial video, height makes most cars and people tiny — so t is often high.
Illustration

Say: "The second cue is the share of previous boxes smaller than 32 by 32 pixels. Seven tiny boxes out of ten gives 0.7."

Likely question: "Where does 32×32 come from?" — it is the widely used small-object size from COCO-style evaluation; we state openly that it is an external convention, not a tuned value.

Section IV · Temporal stability

Smoothing: Ignore One Strange Frame

“One difficult frame should not immediately change the whole detector configuration.”
What happens

Reading 3 jumps to 0.80. The smoothed SCI only rises to 0.44, then settles back — the detector settings do not flicker.

Benefit

stable settings, stable IDs

Cost

a small delay when the scene really changes

Illustrative SCI readings

Say: "If SCI jumped on every strange frame, the input size and thresholds would switch back and forth, and tracks would suffer. A rolling mean of the last seven readings absorbs a single spike."

Likely question: "Why seven?" — an engineering choice giving about two seconds of history at a 10-frame stride; a 5 × 5 temporal ablation (window 1–9, stride 1–20) was later run to test it, and the frozen studies kept W = 7, S = 10.

Section IV · Temporal stability

Analyze Every 10 Frames, Reuse in Between

Every frame

Reacts fastest, but spends CPU on 30 analyses per second — scenes rarely change that fast.

Every 10 frames (S = 10)

At 30 FPS that is 3 analyses per second. Cheap, and still quick to react.

Window W = 7

7 readings × 10 frames ≈ 2 seconds of recent history at 30 FPS.

Trade-off: checking less often saves compute but reacts later. We chose W = 7 and S = 10, then tested 25 timing settings.

Say: "The analyzer runs on frame 1, 11, 21 and so on. In between, the detector reuses the latest SCI. That keeps the CPU cost small while still reacting several times per second."

Numbers: (7 − 1) × 10 ⁄ 30 = 2 seconds between the oldest and newest reading in the window.

Section VII · Optimization method

Optuna: An Automatic Settings Search

What Optuna is

An open-source hyperparameter optimization framework. It proposes settings, runs the experiment, reads the score, and learns where to look next.

What Optuna is NOT

Not AI in the sense of a trained model, not a neural network, and it does not train YOLO. It only chooses numbers.

TPE sampler · seed 42

Sample where good trials are dense and bad trials are rare. A fixed seed makes the whole search repeatable.

Optuna (Akiba et al., KDD 2019) · TPE (Bergstra et al., NeurIPS 2011)density sketch: illustration

Say: "Instead of trying 0.25, then 0.30, then 0.35 by hand, Optuna proposes whole combinations, runs AC-MOT on the validation videos, reads the scores, and focuses on promising regions."

Likely questions: "Is Optuna AI?" — it is a search tool, not a learned model. "Why TPE?" — it works well with mixed discrete and continuous settings and few trials. "Why 50 trials?" — a computing budget fixed in advance, not a proof of the best possible setting.

Section VIII · Reproduction

Compatibility-Only Fixes for a Modern Stack

The official U2MOT code was written for an older Python / PyTorch / NumPy stack. Four small runtime fixes were needed to run it on PyTorch 2.11 and NumPy 2.1.

torch.load(path, weights_only=False) # PyTorch 2.x blocks old checkpoints by default np.float → float # alias removed in NumPy 2 np.float_ → np.float64 # alias removed in NumPy 2 PYTHONPATH += <repo root> # imports resolve as in the original layout

These changes do NOT modify

  • model weights
  • detector logic
  • tracking logic
  • thresholds
  • data
  • evaluation protocol
They only let the original code run in the modern environment.
Reproduction log as recorded by the author

Say: "To run old code on a new stack I made only compatibility changes — how a checkpoint is loaded, two removed NumPy type names, and the import path. Nothing that could change the results."

Why mention it: reproducibility — a reviewer can check that no algorithmic change slipped in.

Section VIII · Scientific question

Can the Scene Score Transfer to a New Pipeline?

Can the SCI designed and tested with YOLOv8n + ByteTrack help a stronger, different pipeline — YOLOX-X + U2MOT?

Transfer as-is

  • V1 SCI weights (crowd 0.129 · tiny 0.222 · edge 0.434 · night 0.054 · blur 0.161)
  • 7-reading smoothing
  • 10-frame analysis interval

Do NOT copy

  • YOLOv8n confidence / NMS / size values
  • YOLOX-X has a different operating point (e.g. NMS 0.7, confidence 0.09, 1600 × 896)
  • the detector action mapping must be re-fitted on validation data
Planned experiment · only the SCI representation is transferred initially

Say: "The interesting question is what transfers. The idea of measuring scene difficulty — the SCI weights, smoothing and interval — may transfer. The exact detector values do not: a YOLOX-X at confidence 0.09 behaves nothing like a YOLOv8n at 0.30, so the mapping from SCI to settings must be learned again on validation data."

Section IX · Honest limits

Limitations

Hand-designed cues

SCI uses five designed visual cues, not a learned difficulty model.

Empirical thresholds

Some constants (e.g. 32 × 32) are conventions, not learned.

Depends on detections

Crowd and tiny cues use the previous boxes — errors can feed back.

Mapping per detector

A new detector needs its SCI → settings mapping re-fitted on validation.

Latency cost

Adaptive resolution raises latency on hard scenes.

Custom protocol

Results are class-agnostic custom TrackEval, not leaderboard results; COCO has no van class.

U2MOT too heavy

About 5–6 FPS — unsuitable for the main real-time goal.

Scope

Two aerial datasets and one GPU; camera-motion awareness can improve.

Say: "I want to be clear about the limits. The cues are designed, some thresholds are conventions, the mapping must be re-fitted for new detectors, and results use a custom protocol on one GPU."

Tip: naming limitations first usually shortens hostile questions.
